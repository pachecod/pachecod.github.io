U.S. Global Troop Presence Since 1950

An interactive exploration of American military presence across global regions over the past seven decades. Navigate through different regions to discover troop patterns and historical trends.

Overview

Since the end of World War II, the United States has maintained an unprecedented global military presence, with troops stationed across every inhabited continent. At its peak during the Cold War, hundreds of thousands of American service members were stationed abroad, while today that number has stabilized at approximately 160,000 personnel in dozens of countries worldwide. About this project

This pattern reflects major geopolitical shifts over the past seven decades—from Cold War containment strategies and the Korean and Vietnam Wars, to post-9/11 operations in the Middle East, and ongoing commitments to allies in Europe and the Indo-Pacific. The data reveals not just where American troops have been sent, but why: to deter adversaries, support allies, maintain regional stability, and respond to humanitarian crises. By examining troop levels across different regions over time, we can trace the evolving nature of American military engagement and the strategic priorities that have shaped U.S. foreign policy for three generations.

U.S. Troops in East Asia and the Pacific by Year

Total U.S. troops deployed in the East Asia and Pacific region each year, 1950–2024. Countries and territories in this region include American Samoa, Australia, Brunei, Cambodia, China, East Timor, Fiji, Guam, Indonesia, Japan, Kiribati, Laos, Malaysia, Marshall Islands, Micronesia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nauru, New Zealand, North Korea, Palau, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Samoa, Singapore, Solomon Islands, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, Tonga, Tuvalu, US Virgin Islands, Vanuatu, Vietnam, and Wake Island.

Source: Michael Flynn. Data: Michael A. Allen, Michael E. Flynn, and Carla Martinez Machain. 2022. “Global U.S. military deployment data: 1950–2020.” Conflict Management and Peace Science 39(3): 351–370.

The first large spike in troop deployment comes in 1951–55 due to the Korean War. The Korean War was a civil war between the communist North Korea and the capitalist South Korea. North Korea was backed by China and the USSR, while South Korea was backed by the United States and other members of the United Nations. U.S. involvement ramped up when North Korea initially invaded deep into South Korea, which led to more troops being deployed. The war ended in a stalemate when both sides agreed to an armistice in 1953. U.S. troops, along with North and South Korean troops, are still stationed at the Korean Demilitarized Zone, which is the border between the two countries.

The second large spike in troop deployment comes in 1965–72 due to the Vietnam War. The Vietnam War was a civil war between the communist North Vietnam and the capitalist South Vietnam. North Vietnam had support from China, the USSR, and North Korea. South Vietnam had support mainly from the United States. U.S. involvement in the war was very controversial, and public opinion fully turned against the conflict with the release of the Pentagon Papers and other information proving U.S. leaders knew they were losing the war. Both sides also committed many heinous war crimes. The war ended in a North Vietnam victory, with the North and South merging into the Socialist Republic of Vietnam.

South Asia

This map shows the various countries across South Asia that have had US troops since 1950. This map includes Afghanistan, Diego Garcia (a little island that functions as a joint United Kingdom, United States military operations base), India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Bhutan, Kashmir (a disputed territory between Pakistan and India), Maldives, Nepal, Seychelles, and Sri Lanka. It is important to note that Kashmir has only had 1 US troop, according to the data, in 1954. Bhutan is listed in the data, but has had zero troops, according to the data. It is included in the map for unaccounted for military presence and is subject to be removed or edited. The most troops have been in Afghanistan, followed by Diego Garcia, then Pakistan, and then India. An interesting angle to see take shape is how the presence of nuclear weapons impacts the presence of US troops, such as in India and Pakistan.

The most US troops in a specific country within South Asia during one year was in Afghanistan in 2011 with 82,177 troops. Generally, the highest number of US troops in South Asia was in Afghanistan during the 2000s, the spike occurring in 2001 which was a result of the 9/11 terrorist attack. After the attack, the US declared war and stationed troops in Afghanistan as a part of “Operation Enduring Freedom,” which was aimed to remove the Taliban and dismantle al-Qaeda. US presence and troops in Afghanistan stayed for a long time, with the US finally withdrawing in 2021. There are no US troops actively in Afghanistan.

Credit: Michael Flynn, BBC.com, cfr.org

The War in Afghanistan started in 2001 following the terror attacks on September 11th committed by al-Qaeda. This spiked the graph in 2001 at 2,000 troops and steadily increased in the ensuing years. Even when the Taliban, a close ally of al-Qaeda, was pushed into retreat in 2002, in 2005 they resurged and escalated attacks with suicide bombings, causing increased military presence.

During Obama’s administration there was a large effort to increase military presence in response to the faltering effort in Afghanistan. This leads to the biggest spikes in the graph during the period of 2009–2011 (peaking in 2011 at 82,177), with 2011 being the year that Osama bin Laden, the founder of al-Qaeda, was killed by U.S. forces. This and other operations to disrupt al-Qaeda would lead to a decreased military presence, as shown by the graph decreasing.

The Taliban and the U.S. would still be at odds for many years, but the U.S. could not defeat the Taliban militarily, leading to the Doha Agreement in 2020 where the U.S. agreed to withdraw. The graph portrays this at the very end where the military presence becomes close to 0 in Afghanistan, leaving an opening in 2021 for the Taliban to recapture Kabul.

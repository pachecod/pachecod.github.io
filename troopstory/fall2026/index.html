U.S. Global Troop Presence Since 1950

An interactive exploration of American military presence across global regions over the past seven decades. Navigate through different regions to discover troop patterns and historical trends.

East Asia Europe Latin America Middle East North America South Asia Africa

Overview

Since the end of World War II, the United States has maintained an unprecedented global military presence, with troops stationed across every inhabited continent. At its peak during the Cold War, hundreds of thousands of American service members were stationed abroad, while today that number has stabilized at approximately 160,000 personnel in dozens of countries worldwide. About this project

This pattern reflects major geopolitical shifts over the past seven decades—from Cold War containment strategies and the Korean and Vietnam Wars, to post-9/11 operations in the Middle East, and ongoing commitments to allies in Europe and the Indo-Pacific. The data reveals not just where American troops have been sent, but why: to deter adversaries, support allies, maintain regional stability, and respond to humanitarian crises. By examining troop levels across different regions over time, we can trace the evolving nature of American military engagement and the strategic priorities that have shaped U.S. foreign policy for three generations.

East Asia & Pacific

By Katherine Schlueter and Mark Rodeghero

The United States military has had a significant presence in East Asia and the Pacific for much of our history. Chief conflicts include fighting Japan in World War II, North Korea in the Korean War, and the North Vietnamese during the Vietnam War. The data does not include the U.S. colonization of the Pacific in the 19th century and World War II.

Prior to World War II, the U.S. colonized the Kingdom of Hawaii, Guam, the Philippines (until they gained independence in 1946), and American Samoa. In the wake of World War II, the United Nations assigned the United States administrative control across strategic territories in Micronesia, incorporating areas now recognized as the Marshall Islands, Northern Mariana Islands, and the Federated States of Micronesia.

U.S. Troops in East Asia and the Pacific by Year

Total U.S. troops deployed in the East Asia and Pacific region each year, 1950–2024. Countries and territories in this region include American Samoa, Australia, Brunei, Cambodia, China, East Timor, Fiji, Guam, Indonesia, Japan, Kiribati, Laos, Malaysia, Marshall Islands, Micronesia, Mongolia, Myanmar, Nauru, New Zealand, North Korea, Palau, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Samoa, Singapore, Solomon Islands, South Korea, Taiwan, Thailand, Tonga, Tuvalu, U.S. Virgin Islands, Vanuatu, Vietnam, and Wake Island.

Source: Michael Flynn. Data: Michael A. Allen, Michael E. Flynn, and Carla Martinez Machain. 2022. “Global U.S. military deployment data: 1950–2020.” Conflict Management and Peace Science 39(3): 351–370.

The first large spike in troop deployment comes in 1951–55 due to the Korean War. The Korean War was a civil war between the communist North Korea and the capitalist South Korea. North Korea was backed by China and the USSR, while South Korea was backed by the United States and other members of the United Nations. U.S. involvement ramped up when North Korea initially invaded deep into South Korea, which led to more troops being deployed. The war ended in a stalemate when both sides agreed to an armistice in 1953. U.S. troops, along with North and South Korean troops, are still stationed at the Korean Demilitarized Zone, which is the border between the two countries.

The second large spike in troop deployment comes in 1965–72 due to the Vietnam War. The Vietnam War was a civil war between the communist North Vietnam and the capitalist South Vietnam. North Vietnam had support from China, the USSR, and North Korea. South Vietnam had support mainly from the United States. U.S. involvement in the war was very controversial, and public opinion fully turned against the conflict with the release of the Pentagon Papers and other information proving U.S. leaders knew they were losing the war. Both sides also committed many war crimes. The war ended in a North Vietnam victory, with the North and South merging into the Socialist Republic of Vietnam.

Japan was defeated on V-J Day in 1945, shortly after President Truman dropped two nuclear bombs on Japan, resulting in the death of an estimated 210,000 to over 340,000 people in Hiroshima and Nagasaki. To rebuild Japan in the aftermath of the war, the United States has maintained a large military presence in Japan for the past 50 years.

The bar chart race shows how the U.S. presence in East Asia and the Pacific shifted from country to country. Japan had the most U.S. troops in the region in 55 of the 75 years in the data and South Korea in 12. Vietnam topped the region from 1965 to 1972, peaking at about 537,000 U.S. troops in 1968. In 2024, Japan hosted about 54,800 U.S. troops, South Korea about 24,200 and Guam about 6,700.

Europe & Central Asia

By Ava Rabinovich, Macey Kim and Ben Belford-Peltzman

Europe has hosted one of the largest concentrations of U.S. troops abroad since 1950, a legacy of the Cold War and the NATO alliance. U.S. troop numbers in Europe and Central Asia peaked at about 441,000 in 1957. By 2024, about 68,000 active-duty U.S. troops were stationed across 49 countries in the region.

Troop levels stayed high through the Cold War, with about 291,000 U.S. troops still in the region in 1990. After the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, numbers fell quickly, to about 110,000 by 1995. Germany has always been the center of the U.S. presence in Europe: troop numbers there peaked at about 274,000 in 1962 and stood at about 35,000 in 2024. Italy (12,456), the United Kingdom (10,180) and Spain (3,586) had the next-largest deployments in 2024. In Central Asia, U.S. troop numbers rose after 2001 to support the war in Afghanistan; Kyrgyzstan, home to a U.S. transit hub, hosted about 10,200 U.S. troops at the peak in 2011.

Latin America & Caribbean

By Gabby Guadalupe, Mikayla Gallias and Alexandra Bernard

U.S. troop levels in Latin America and the Caribbean were highest in the early 1950s, peaking at about 49,000 in 1953, when large garrisons were stationed in Puerto Rico and the Panama Canal Zone. Numbers declined over the following decades, to about 5,000 in 2000, the year after the United States handed control of the Panama Canal to Panama, and to about 2,400 in 2024. Short-term interventions also show up in the data: Haiti hosted about 17,500 U.S. troops in 1994.

This map shows how many active-duty U.S. troops were stationed in Latin America in 2024. Excluding Puerto Rico, a U.S. territory, and Mexico, which is classified as part of North America, the region hosted 1,635 troops in total. Those troops were concentrated in just a few places. Cuba had the most with 616, followed by Honduras with 365 and Colombia with 67. Cuba and Honduras together account for 60% of the regional total. Cuba’s figure reflects the U.S. naval base at Guantánamo Bay, which has been in place for more than a century. The rest of the region has far smaller numbers, with most countries hosting fewer than 70 troops.

U.S. troop numbers in Cuba have gone up and down sharply since 1950. The count started just below 2,000 and climbed to a peak of 11,887 in 1956, then fell to 2,853 by 1959. A short spike came in the mid-1990s, but numbers have since generally declined. In 2024 there were 616 troops in Cuba, about 95% less than the peak, though Cuba still has more U.S. troops than any other place in Latin America (excluding Puerto Rico and Mexico).

The biggest changes in U.S. troop numbers in Cuba line up with major political events. The count peaked at 11,887 in 1956, the year Fidel Castro began his armed campaign. Troops also rose in 1962 during the Cuban Missile Crisis, and a final spike came in the mid-1990s with Guantánamo Bay.

Middle East & North Africa

By Chris Boylan, Harry Brubaker and Julia Arbelaez

The top five Middle East countries with the most U.S. troops in 2024. Bahrain had the most total troops, with nearly 3,500, followed by Kuwait, Djibouti, Qatar, and Saudi Arabia. None of the countries other than Bahrain had over 550 troops.

The U.S. military presence in the Middle East and North Africa was small for much of the Cold War, with about 3,000 troops in the region in 1950 and about 33,000 in 1990. That changed with the wars in the Persian Gulf and Iraq. Regional troop numbers peaked at about 223,000 in 2007, the year of the troop surge in Iraq, and fell to about 5,700 by 2024, most of them in Bahrain, home of the U.S. Navy’s Fifth Fleet.

This map displays the top five locations where the United States had the most troops in 2024. The map illustrates the geographic distribution of U.S. military deployments and related incidents across the Middle East, with most activity occurring around the Persian Gulf. Countries such as Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Djibouti, and Bahrain have played important roles in U.S. military operations, serving as locations for troop deployments and military bases. The U.S. had large deployments in those countries to deter regional aggression, protect global trade, and use these countries as strategic military hubs.

In Iraq, U.S. troop numbers jumped from zero in 2002 to 136,000 in 2003, the year of the U.S.-led invasion, and peaked at 170,000 in 2007 during the troop surge. Numbers fell steadily after that, to about 28,700 in 2011 and to 164 in 2012, after U.S. forces withdrew at the end of 2011. In Afghanistan, U.S. troop levels peaked later, at 82,177 in 2011.

North America

By Dan Pacheco

North America is different from every other region in this project because it includes the United States itself. In 2024, about 1.13 million active-duty troops were stationed in the U.S., compared with 156 in Canada and 143 in Greenland, home of Pituffik Space Base, formerly Thule Air Base. Because troops stationed at home are not deployed overseas, the U.S. figures are best read separately from the foreign deployments shown elsewhere in this story.

U.S.-based troop numbers peaked at about 2.3 million in 1953, during the Korean War, and stood at about 1.13 million in 2024. U.S. troop levels in Canada and Greenland peaked in the 1950s, when the U.S. operated air and radar bases in both places: about 18,300 in Canada in 1957 and about 8,350 in Greenland in 1955. In 2024, each hosted fewer than 200 U.S. troops.

South Asia

By Ethan Higby and Lizzie Lindsey

This map shows the various countries across South Asia that have had U.S. troops since 1950. This map includes Afghanistan, Diego Garcia (a little island that functions as a joint United Kingdom, United States military operations base), India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Kashmir (a disputed territory between Pakistan and India), Maldives, Nepal, Seychelles, and Sri Lanka. It is important to note that Kashmir has only had one U.S. troop, according to the data, in 1954. The most troops have been in Afghanistan, followed by Diego Garcia, then Pakistan, and then India. An interesting angle to see take shape is how the presence of nuclear weapons impacts the presence of U.S. troops, such as in India and Pakistan.

The most U.S. troops in a specific country within South Asia during one year was in Afghanistan in 2011 with 82,177 troops. Generally, the highest number of U.S. troops in South Asia was in Afghanistan during the 2000s, the spike occurring in 2001, which was a result of the 9/11 terrorist attack. After the attack, the U.S. declared war and stationed troops in Afghanistan as a part of “Operation Enduring Freedom,” which was aimed to remove the Taliban and dismantle al-Qaeda. U.S. presence and troops in Afghanistan stayed for a long time, with the U.S. finally withdrawing in 2021. There are no U.S. troops actively in Afghanistan.

Credit: Michael Flynn, BBC.com, cfr.org

The War in Afghanistan started in 2001 following the terror attacks on September 11th committed by al-Qaeda. This spiked the graph in 2001 at 2,000 troops and steadily increased in the ensuing years. Even when the Taliban, a close ally of al-Qaeda, was pushed into retreat in 2002, in 2005 they resurged and escalated attacks with suicide bombings, causing increased military presence.

During Obama’s administration there was a large effort to increase military presence in response to the faltering effort in Afghanistan. This led to the biggest spikes in the graph during the period of 2009–2011 (peaking in 2011 at 82,177), with 2011 being the year that Osama bin Laden, the founder of al-Qaeda, was killed by U.S. forces. This and other operations to disrupt al-Qaeda would lead to a decreased military presence, as shown by the graph decreasing.

The Taliban and the U.S. would still be at odds for many years, but the U.S. could not defeat the Taliban militarily, leading to the Doha Agreement in 2020 where the U.S. agreed to withdraw. The graph portrays this at the very end where the military presence becomes close to 0 in Afghanistan, leaving an opening in 2021 for the Taliban to recapture Kabul.

U.S. troop presence in Pakistan in the 1960s, showing 1955–1975.

The chart shows a buildup of U.S. troops in Pakistan during the 1960s, when Pakistan was a Cold War ally of the United States. Troop numbers rose from a few hundred in the late 1950s to a peak of 1,589 in 1968, then dropped to 658 in 1969 and to 24 in 1970, the year the U.S. ended operations at its air station near Peshawar.

Sub-Saharan Africa

By Toph McWilliams and Andrew Youngworth

Sub-Saharan Africa has had the smallest U.S. military presence of any region in this data. Apart from 1993, when about 6,600 U.S. troops were in the region, most of them in Somalia, the regional total never exceeded about 2,000. In 2024, about 700 U.S. troops were spread across 41 countries, with the largest groups in Kenya (72), Somalia (60) and South Africa (48).

The map shows the locations of countries and territories in Sub-Saharan Africa where the United States has stationed troops. U.S. military personnel are spread across much of the region. The map highlights the geographic reach of the U.S. military presence with deployments in countries such as Somalia, Kenya, and Djibouti’s neighboring region. The United States maintains a military presence across Africa to support operations and security partnerships.

The chart shows U.S. troops stationed in Sub-Saharan Africa from 1990–1999. In December 1992, the U.S. deployed approximately 25,000 troops to help secure humanitarian aid deliveries. In 1993, the Battle of Mogadishu was responsible for a spike in the number of troops deployed in regions like Somalia.
